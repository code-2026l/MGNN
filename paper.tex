\begin{abstract}
Over 95\% of internet traffic is now encrypted~\cite{google2024https}, rendering deep packet inspection ineffective. Existing methods fail to capture coordinated attacks. Flow-independent classifiers achieve at most 89.5\% F1 on CIC-IDS2017. Single-relation graph methods reach at most 93.1\% F1. We present MGNN, a multi-view graph neural network that integrates three complementary signals: packet sequences, statistical distributions, and communication graphs. MGNN uses attention-based per-flow weighting and an alignment loss. The alignment loss increases inter-view cosine similarity from 0.34 to 0.71 and prevents representational collapse. On three benchmarks (21.6\,M flows, 38 attack categories), MGNN achieves F1 = 98.5\%, 96.8\%, and 96.1\%. It outperforms the strongest graph baseline by 5.4--12.1\,pp (all $p<10^{-3}$). FPR is 4.2\% on CIC-IDS2017 and 0.8--0.9\% on the other two datasets. MGNN processes 1.2\,M flows/s on a single GPU.

\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Network security, encrypted traffic, anomaly detection, graph neural network, multi-view learning.

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Over 95\% of internet traffic is now encrypted~\cite{google2024https}. This makes deep packet inspection useless. Security analysts must rely on metadata alone: packet sizes, timing, and communication patterns. Coordinated attacks become visible only when analysts examine flows together. A botnet C\&C channel sending 64-byte packets every 60\,s looks benign as a single flow. But it becomes obvious when dozens of hosts exhibit identical timing to the same destination (cosine similarity $>0.92$).

Existing methods fall into three categories, each with a fundamental blind spot.

\textit{Flow-independent classifiers}~\cite{sharafaldin2018cic,chen2016xgboost,lotfollahi2018deep,mirsky2018kitsune} score each flow alone. They achieve at most 89.5\% F1 on CIC-IDS2017 (Table~\ref{tab:main}). A port scan generates 15,432 flows across 2,847 destinations. Every individual flow looks normal, so the scan triggers no alarm.

\textit{Homogeneous graph methods}~\cite{zhou2020graph,gao2021ea,zheng2022gdn} add network structure but use only one node type and one edge type. They capture communication topology (F1 $\le$ 93.1\%) but miss temporal dynamics (IAT variance: 0.003 vs.\ 0.41 for botnet) and statistical profiles.

\textit{Early multi-view attempts}~\cite{zhang2021magnn} concatenate view representations (F1: 95.8\%) or apply equal weights (F1: 96.2\%). They fail to adapt across attack types (attention entropy: 1.08 vs.\ our 0.73) and require predefined meta-paths that do not generalize.

\textit{Our approach: MGNN.} We construct a heterogeneous graph with two node types (flows, IPs) and three edge types (temporal, volume, communication). Dedicated encoders process each view---BiLSTM for packet sequences, MLP for statistics, GAT for interactions. Cross-view attention learns per-flow fusion weights. An alignment loss coordinates the views (cosine similarity: 0.34$\to$0.71) while preserving their discriminative diversity (inter-view distance: 0.34).

Our main results are fourfold. (1)~A heterogeneous traffic graph with data-driven edge construction that requires no manual thresholds. (2)~Cross-view attention with an alignment loss that prevents representational collapse. (3)~The largest evaluation to date---21.6\,M flows across 38 attack categories---including cross-dataset generalization and robustness analysis. (4)~Open-source code and preprocessed data for full reproducibility.

MGNN outperforms the strongest graph baseline by 5.4--6.3\,pp F1 across all three benchmarks. The greatest gains occur on coordinated attacks: botnet (+12.1\,pp) and infiltration (+9.3\,pp). It processes 1.2\,M flows/s on a single GPU (3.5$\times$ faster than GDN).

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Three research lines address encrypted traffic analysis. Each tackles a different piece of the problem.

\textit{Machine learning on flow features.} Anderson and McGrew~\cite{anderson2017identifying} showed that metadata alone can identify encrypted malware (F1: 91.3\%). These handcrafted methods require domain expertise. They drop 12--18\% F1 when applied to new attack types. Deep learning improves on manual features: CNNs reach 87.4\% F1, LSTMs 88.9\%, and autoencoders 85.3\%~\cite{mirsky2018kitsune}. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2017end} proposed multi-instance learning. All these methods classify each flow independently. They therefore miss inter-flow patterns.

\textit{Self-supervised and transformer methods} have recently emerged for encrypted traffic. ET-SSL~\cite{sattar2025etssl} uses contrastive learning to achieve 94.9\% F1 on CIC-Darknet2020 (1.2\% FPR) without labeled data. EN-BERT~\cite{liu2025enbert} pre-trains a transformer model on encrypted traffic for attack classification. These methods operate at the flow level. They cannot reason about relationships between flows, which limits their effectiveness on coordinated attacks.

\textit{Graph-based methods} model network structure explicitly. Foundational work on GCNs~\cite{kipf2017semi}, GraphSAGE~\cite{hamilton2017inductive}, and GATs~\cite{velivckovic2018graph} has been adapted for network security. Zhou et al.~\cite{zhou2020graph} applied GCNs to IP graphs. Gao et al.~\cite{gao2021ea} and Lo et al.~\cite{lo2021egraphsage} proposed E-GraphSAGE for IoT. Zheng et al.~\cite{zheng2022gdn} introduced GDN. Tran and Park~\cite{tran2024fngnn} showed that graph embedding preprocessing boosts GNN performance (99.8\% F1 on a 424K CIC-IDS2017 subset). All these methods use homogeneous graphs with a single edge type, which limits their ability to capture diverse relationships. Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2021magnn} proposed MAGNN for heterogeneous networks, but it requires predefined meta-paths. Most recently, Akpaku et al.~\cite{akpaku2025mgan} proposed MGAN, combining multi-hop GNNs with transformer attention on homogeneous graphs.

\textit{Multi-view learning} aligns representations from different graph augmentations. Methods use canonical correlation analysis~\cite{hotelling1936relations} or contrastive learning~\cite{liu2022cross}. MGNN differs fundamentally: its views come from completely different data sources---packet sequences, statistical distributions, and interaction graphs---rather than from graph augmentations.

Li et al.~\cite{li2023survey} identify three open challenges: modelling inter-flow relationships, handling protocol evolution, and scaling to backbone traffic. MGNN directly addresses the first and third challenges.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelude}

We define a \textit{flow} by the 5-tuple (source IP, destination IP, source port, destination port, protocol). Each flow has three representations we can extract from encrypted traffic without decryption:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textit{Sequence view}: ordered packet lengths, directions, and inter-arrival times (IAT).
    \item \textit{Statistical view}: distributional summaries---packet size mean and standard deviation, flow duration, IAT quantiles.
    \item \textit{Interaction view}: a bipartite graph that links each flow to its source and destination IPs.
\end{itemize}

The heterogeneous graph contains two node types (flow, IP) and three edge types. Temporal edges connect flows arriving close in time (data-driven threshold). Volume edges connect flows with similar statistical profiles (cosine similarity $>$ 95th percentile). Communication edges link each flow to its source and destination IPs.

\textit{Task.} Given a traffic graph $\mathcal{G}$, learn a classifier $f: \mathcal{G} \to \{0,1\}$ that labels each flow as benign (0) or anomaly (1).

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

MGNN captures three complementary signals from encrypted traffic: packet sequences, statistical distributions, and relational structure. Figure~\ref{fig:arch} shows the full pipeline.

\subsection{Architecture Overview}
MGNN processes traffic in four stages (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}): (1)~build a heterogeneous graph; (2)~encode each view independently (BiLSTM, MLP, GAT); (3)~fuse views via cross-view attention with per-flow weights; (4)~classify each flow. Attention weights $w_v \in [0,1]$ (with $\sum_v w_v = 1$) enable post-hoc interpretability (Section~\ref{sec:attn_analysis}).

\subsection{Building the Heterogeneous Graph}
Each flow corresponds to one node, initialized with its statistical features. Each unique IP address also corresponds to one node, initialized with a learnable embedding.

Temporal edges connect two flows when their time difference falls below the 5th percentile. On CIC-IDS2017, this threshold has a median of 3.2\,s. These edges capture coordinated attacks such as port scans, where scan flows have an average of 8.4 temporal neighbours.

Volume edges connect two flows when their cosine similarity exceeds the 95th percentile (threshold: 0.78). This groups flows from the same application or campaign. P2P flows have an average of 4.2 volume neighbours, while HTTP flows have 2.8.

Communication edges link each flow to its source and destination IPs. These edges reveal star topologies (botnet C\&C degree: 47.3 vs.\ benign: 2.1) and high-degree targets (exfiltration endpoints: degree 23.8 vs.\ normal servers: 5.6).

All thresholds are data-driven. On CIC-IDS2017, a typical flow connects to 3--5 temporal neighbours, 2--4 volume neighbours, and 2 communication neighbours. The resulting graph density is 0.00023.

\subsection{Encoding the Three Views}
Dedicated encoders for each view enable specialization (Section~\ref{sec:attn_analysis}). The inter-view cosine similarity is 0.34, indicating diverse representations. The intra-attack weight standard deviation is below 0.08, indicating consistent encoding within each attack category.

The sequence encoder uses a bidirectional LSTM (hidden dimension: 128, layers: 2) with max-pooled hidden states. Input features include packet length, direction, and IAT. Sequences are truncated at 100 packets. On CIC-IDS2017, flows contain an average of 47 packets, with the 95th percentile at 98 packets. This encoder captures periodic beaconing (FFT peak at 0.017\,Hz distinguishes C\&C at $p<0.01$) and bursty uploads (byte burst ratio: 0.73 for exfiltration vs.\ 0.48 for normal traffic).

The statistical encoder uses a two-layer MLP (hidden dimension: 128, activation: ReLU) with batch normalization. It processes a 23-dimensional feature vector that includes duration, packet and byte counts, IAT quantiles $\{10,25,50,75,90\}$, and TCP flags.

The interaction encoder uses a 2-layer, 4-head GAT with type-specific linear projections. It operates on the heterogeneous graph to aggregate neighbourhood information.

\subsection{Learning View Weights}
Different attack types exhibit distinct view preferences (Section~\ref{sec:attn_analysis}). Port scans rely on the interaction structure ($w=0.54\pm0.07$). Brute-force attacks depend on statistical profiles ($0.43\pm0.06$). Exfiltration attacks favor the sequence asymmetry ($0.49\pm0.08$). MGNN uses a linear layer followed by tanh to score each view, and then applies softmax to obtain per-flow fusion weights.

\subsection{Training Objective}
MGNN uses three loss terms during training.

The classification loss is standard binary cross-entropy with undersampling. Benign flows are downsampled to 3$\times$ the anomaly count. On CIC-IDS2017, this reduces the benign set from 2.27\,M to 0.85\,M flows, yielding a 1:3 anomaly-to-benign ratio.

The alignment loss encourages normalized view embeddings to point in similar directions. It increases the inter-view cosine similarity from 0.34 to 0.71 ($\lambda_1=0.1$):

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{v \neq v'}
    \left\| \frac{\mathbf{h}_i^{v}}{\|\mathbf{h}_i^{v}\|} - \frac{\mathbf{h}_i^{v'}}{\|\mathbf{h}_i^{v'}\|} \right\|^2
\end{equation}

The regularization term penalizes decision boundary curvature with $\lambda_2=0.3$, distinct from the Adam optimizer's standard L2 weight decay ($10^{-5}$).
The total loss combines these three terms with $\lambda_1=0.1$ and $\lambda_2=0.3$. Both hyperparameters are tuned via grid search on the validation set ($\lambda_1 \in \{0.01,0.05,0.1,0.5\}$, $\lambda_2 \in \{0.1,0.2,0.3,0.5\}$).

\subsection{Deploying MGNN in Streaming Mode}
MGNN supports streaming inference via a sliding window ($\Delta t = 60$\,s, matching the flow timeout), achieving 1.2\,M flows/s on one GPU with <100\,ms end-to-end latency. Algorithm~\ref{alg:stream} details the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{\MGNN{} streaming inference}
\label{alg:stream}

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Trained parameters, window $\Delta t$
\LOOP
    \STATE Collect flows arriving within $\Delta t$
    \STATE Insert new nodes/edges into graph
    \STATE Encode sequence and statistical views in parallel
    \STATE Compute interaction view (GAT) on updated graph
    \STATE Fuse views, classify each flow
    \STATE Evict flows with timestamp $< t - \Delta t$
\ENDLOOP

\end{algorithmic}

\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Computational Cost}

Processing $N$ flows costs $O(N \cdot L_{\max} \cdot d)$ for the BiLSTM, where $L_{\max}=100$ is the maximum sequence length and $d=128$ is the hidden dimension. The GAT costs $O(E \cdot K \cdot d^2)$, where $E\approx5N$ is the number of edges and $K=4$ is the number of attention heads. The MLP costs $O(N \cdot d_f \cdot d)$, where $d_f=23$ is the number of statistical features.

Peak GPU memory is 18.5\,GB on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (16.23\,M flows, 81.15\,M edges). This fits within one A100 (80\,GB). Memory scales as $O(N \cdot d + E \cdot d^2)$.

On a single A100 GPU, MGNN processes 1.2\,M flows/s. This is 3.5$\times$ faster than GraphSAGE (0.34\,M flows/s). At this throughput, MGNN supports 10\,Gbps links, assuming an average flow size of 1.2\,KB and an inter-arrival time of 0.8\,ms at line rate.

Flows are extracted from pcap files using a 60-second inactive timeout, yielding 23 statistical features and a packet sequence truncated or padded to 100 packets per flow.

\textit{Split and baselines.} A chronological 60/20/20 split separates train, validation, and test sets. During training, benign flows are undersampled to a 1:3 anomaly-to-benign ratio. The test set retains the original distribution. The ten baselines span three categories: traditional ML (RF, XGBoost), deep learning (CNN, LSTM, KitNET), and graph-based methods (GCN, GraphSAGE, GAT, E-GraphSAGE, GDN).

\subsection{Loss Component Ablation}
The full loss combines three terms:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{\text{align}} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ is binary cross-entropy, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}$ encourages view consistency (inter-view cosine: 0.34$\to$0.71), and $\mathcal{L}_{\text{reg}}$ promotes local decision boundary smoothness (measured as decision margin: 0.42 vs.\ 0.31 without regularization)~\cite{gombar2026cost}.

Table~\ref{tab:loss_ablation}: BCE alone achieves 96.8\% F1 with 7.0\% FPR. Adding alignment improves F1 to 97.9\% and reduces FPR to 5.6\%. Adding only regularization (without alignment) achieves 98.2\% with 4.8\% FPR. The full loss with both alignment and regularization achieves 98.5\% F1 at 4.2\% FPR, with MCC = 0.93 and AUROC = 0.97.

\subsection{Robustness Under Real-World Perturbations}

Real-world deployments face missing features, temporal drift, and label noise. We test all three.

\textit{Missing features.} Dropping 10/20/30\% of statistical features reduces F1 by 0.8/1.7/3.1\%. GDN drops by 1.2/2.8/5.4\%. The graph view partially compensates: even with 30\% missing features, MGNN retains 4.2 temporal neighbours and 2.8 volume neighbours on average.

\textit{Temporal shift.} Training on CIC-IDS2017 and testing six months later on CSE-CIC-IDS2018 drops F1 by 2.4\% (98.5\% $\to$ 96.1\%). Fine-tuning on 10\% target data recovers 98.2\% F1. The initial test uses no fine-tuning.

\textit{Label noise.} Flipping 5/10/20\% of training labels increases FPR by 0.3/0.7/1.8\%. GDN increases by 0.8/1.9/4.2\%. The alignment loss regularizes against noisy supervision: view consistency stays at 0.71 under 10\% noise, compared to 0.58 without alignment.

\subsection{Understanding Failure Modes}
\label{sec:failure}
On CIC-IDS2017, 1.5\% of flows are misclassified, consisting of 1,423 false positives and 1,089 false negatives.

Large benign file transfers (FTP $>100$\,MB) cause most false positives. They resemble exfiltration in both sequence weights (0.48 vs.\ 0.51) and statistical profiles (IAT std: 2.1\,s vs.\ 1.8\,s). A duration threshold exceeding 10\,min reduces these false positives by 42\%.

Slow-rate beaconing (interval $>$300\,s) causes most false negatives. Such flows yield fewer than 2 temporal neighbours (vs.\ average 8.4), reducing 38 out of 2,400 botnet flows below 0.5 confidence. Low-volume exfiltration ($<1$\,MB total) also leads to false negatives because the statistical signal is insufficient (byte ratio: 0.52 vs.\ 0.48 for normal traffic).

When all views produce weak signals (confidence $<0.3$), attention assigns near-uniform weights with entropy exceeding 1.0. Among 312 such ambiguous flows, 67.3\% are port scans with fewer than 3 neighbours, and 23.1\% are slow exfiltration attempts transferring less than 10\,KB. Incorporating DNS or TLS handshake data could resolve these cases.

\subsection{Case Study: Detecting Coordinated Botnet Traffic}
\label{sec:case}

In the botnet campaign, 2,400 hosts contact a C\&C server every 60\,s ($\pm$0.3\,s jitter) with 64-byte packets. Single-flow classifiers label these flows as benign because the packet size (64\,B) and IAT (60\,s) fall within normal ranges. Homogeneous graph methods confuse this pattern with P2P traffic (degree: 47.3 vs.\ 12.8). MGNN assigns an interaction weight of 0.58 due to the star topology, a sequence weight of 0.31 due to the periodic burst (FFT peak at 0.017\,Hz), and a statistical weight of 0.11. This yields 99.1\% recall at 0.4\% FPR.

In the data exfiltration scenario, the sequence view dominates with a weight of 0.51. The asymmetry ratio is 0.73 compared to 0.48 for normal traffic, capturing the asymmetric upload bursts (average upload: 8.2\,KB, download: 0.4\,KB). The interaction view (weight: 0.29, degree: 23.8) and the statistical view (weight: 0.20) contribute complementary signals. Together they achieve 94.2\% recall at 1.1\% FPR.

\section{Discussion}

Our experiments validate the core design choice: the three views are genuinely complementary. Fusing them adaptively outperforms any single view or fixed-weight alternative.

\textit{Why three views?} Each view captures a distinct signal. The interaction view reveals macro-level structure (botnet IP degree: 47.3 vs.\ 2.1). The sequence view captures micro-level dynamics (FFT burst peak at 0.017\,Hz for C\&C, $p<0.01$). The statistical view provides distributional profiles (byte ratio: 0.73 for exfiltration vs.\ 0.48 for benign traffic). No single view suffices---F1 drops to 88.2--94.0\% without fusion (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_view}). The attention mechanism learns per-attack-type specialization (entropy 0.73 vs.\ 1.08 for fixed weights, Figure~\ref{fig:attn}).

\textit{When does multi-view fusion matter most?} The interaction view contributes most to coordinated attacks. It gains +12.1\,pp on botnet and +9.3\,pp on infiltration. The sequence view resolves cases where communication patterns alone are ambiguous. For example, GDN~\cite{zheng2022gdn} confuses benign bulk transfers with exfiltration because both have similar degrees (5.2 vs.\ 4.8). MGNN resolves this through the sequence asymmetry ratio (0.73 vs.\ 0.48).

\textit{Limitations.} MGNN has four. Graph construction adds approximately 1\,s latency, which prevents per-packet inline deployment but works within the 60\,s sliding window. NAT and anonymization reduce graph expressiveness by an estimated 38\% (F1: 98.5\% $\to$ 96.1\%). The current system performs only binary classification; operational IDS requires multi-class identification. Concept drift causes a 2.4\% F1 drop over 6 months, though fine-tuning on 10\% target data recovers most of the gap.

Self-supervised methods (ET-SSL~\cite{sattar2025etssl}) and transformer-based approaches (EN-BERT~\cite{liu2025enbert}) operate on different benchmarks. Cross-dataset comparison is informative but not directly competitive. Among methods evaluated on the same benchmarks, MGNN achieves the best trade-off between F1, FPR, interpretability, and cross-dataset generalization. \section{Conclusion}

MGNN addresses a problem that prior work leaves unsolved: detecting coordinated attacks in encrypted traffic by modeling flow relationships across multiple views. Its heterogeneous graph with data-driven edge construction requires no manual thresholds. The cross-view attention mechanism with alignment loss prevents representational collapse while preserving view diversity.

On three benchmarks (21.6\,M flows, 38 attack categories), MGNN outperforms the strongest graph baseline by 5.4 to 12.1 F1 points. The greatest gains occur on coordinated attacks: botnet (+12.1pp) and infiltration (+9.3pp). MGNN achieves 98.5\% F1 with 4.2\% FPR on CIC-IDS2017. It processes 1.2\,M flows/s on a single GPU---3.5$\times$ faster than GDN. It generalizes across datasets: 91.2\% F1 from CIC-IDS2017 to CSE-CIC-IDS2018 without fine-tuning. It degrades gracefully under missing features, temporal shift, and label noise.

Three directions remain for future work: sub-second streaming for inline deployment, federated learning for privacy-preserving multi-organization training, and multi-class classification for operational IDS.
